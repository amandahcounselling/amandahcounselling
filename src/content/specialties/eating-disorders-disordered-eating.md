You don't need a diagnosis to seek support or to feel like your relationship to food and body is "bad enough" to get help.

If you're living with an eating disorder or disordered eating, you may:
• Over-eat food to self-sooth when you're feeling anxious, stressed, bored, sad, angry, etc.
• Hide food or eat food in secret
• Spend minutes, maybe hours, checking your body in the mirror
• Try to limit the amount of food you eat in a day, and you may bypass or no longer have hunger cues
• Over-exercise, throw up, or use other strategies to compensate for what you ate
• Check your weight on the scale multiple times per week, maybe even daily, and any fluctuations impact your mood and self-confidence
• Have a very strict diet and exercise plan, eating only foods you deem "healthy"
• Avoid environmentss where food may be present or your body may be seen (e.g., not going to restaurants or avoiding pools and beaches)
• Go through phases where your relationship to food and body feels stable, but then a few months later it feels like it's taken over your life again
• Keep your challenges a secret from everyone you know, leaving you feeling a deep sense of loneliness and shame

Disordered eating is a term used when someone doesn't meet diagnostic criteria for an eating disorder. This could look like your symptoms or behaviours not being seen as severe enough for an eating disorder diagnosis. However, this does not mean that your relationship to food and body isn't causing you a significant amount of distress. Your pain and struggle is real, whether you have a diagnosis or not. 

Through my training and work, I began to understand that eating disorders and disordered eating may be tools people used to cope with overwhelming experiences in their lives. I have also found that eating disorders don't typically exist in a vacuum, meaning other factors in our lives impact our relationship to food and our bodies and vice versa. Trauma, interpersonal relationship challenges, and life transitions are common experiences that can often maintain and intensify challenges with food and body image.

This is the "why" behind how I approach counselling for eating disorders and disordered eating. I intentionally creates space to explore your relationship to food and your body, while also looking at other areas of your life that may be impacting your recovery. We will work to understand what the eating disorder/disordered eating may be helping you cope with, while developing new skills to help you turn towards it less, building a life that feels more fulfilling and aligned with who you are.
